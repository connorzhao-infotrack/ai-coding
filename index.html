How much scaffolding do you actually need?

A decision reference for AI-assisted development workflows. Eight levels, from "just type into Claude Code" to "audit-ready AI-DLC." Pick the lightest one that survives the piece of work in front of you. Heavier is not better — heavier is heavier.

🪚 The carpentry view · How AI assists humans, then → now → next

We're not there yet. Most of this scaffolding is temporary.

Building software with AI in 2026 is like working with an apprentice carpenter — good hands, follows blueprints, needs supervision. Most of the heavy scaffolding in this doc exists because the apprentice isn't a foreman yet. As the base models improve, much of it will quietly fade. The question is which parts.

Past · ≤2024

🔧 The Power Drill

A power tool for one specific job. You measure, plan, cut, assemble — the drill just helps with the hole. Useful, but it doesn't know what you're building.

AI was

Autocomplete. Line completion. Snippet search. Copilot in its first form.

Best for

Finishing a syntax pattern you'd already started. Boilerplate. Generating a regex.

Not yet

Anything beyond the line you were typing.

Now · May 2026

🪚 The Apprentice Carpenter

Genuinely useful hands. Cuts straight, joints clean, follows the blueprint — but doesn't yet know why you picked oak over pine. You're the lead carpenter: you decide what gets built, check the joints, catch when the grain runs wrong.

AI is

Pair programmer. Capable junior. Opus 4.7 · GPT-5.5-Codex · agentic, multi-step, persistent context.

Best for

Scoped tickets with clear AC. Refactors. Code-gen from a plan. Test writing. Debug with a stack trace. Greenfield boilerplate.

Not yet

Long-horizon judgment. Ambiguity resolution. "This looks wrong but I can't say why" intuition. Anything regulated or irreversible without a human gate. This is why L0–L4 exist.

Near future · 12–24 months

👷 The Foreman

You're the architect now. The foreman runs the build, manages the crew, comes back to you for big decisions — vendor choices, structural changes, "the client wants a balcony, will it hold?" You brief once, check at milestones, decide on the trade-offs they bring you.

AI will be

Senior peer. Owns features end-to-end. Asks the right clarifying questions before coding.

Best for

Multi-day autonomous work. Whole-feature ownership. Operating a backlog with light human review.

Still not

Replacing human judgment on irreversible calls. Regulated sign-offs. Ethical / commercial / "is this the right thing to build" decisions. Auditors still want a human name.

The honest split · Not all scaffolding is the same Adopt what's durable. Skip what's a crutch.
📉 Will fade · AI-crutch scaffolding

Exists only to compensate for current model limits — memory, focus, planning, instruction-following. Each generation eats one of these.

  • Verbose AGENTS.md reminders ("don't use jQuery again")
  • Plan Mode as an explicit step (will be implicit)
  • Multi-agent handoff protocols (L6) — context drift, hallucination compounding
  • Drift-detection scripts that re-check the spec on every PR
  • "Definition of Ready" gates that exist mostly so the AI doesn't go off-script

Adopting these now is fine — but don't fall in love. The framework you pick today probably won't survive 18 months.

📈 Will survive · Human-coordination scaffolding

Encodes what humans agreed to. A perfect AI doesn't tell future-you why you picked Telnyx — only the written record does. Pre-dates LLMs, post-dates them.

  • ADRs — versioned reasons for each major choice
  • AC discipline — testable "done" definitions per ticket
  • API contracts, JSON schemas, event lists — they're systems-talking-to-systems agreements, not AI scaffolding. Foreman-AI may generate them, but the artifact still exists because the consumer (another team, another service, a regulator) needs a stable interface to bind against
  • Risk floors for high-blast-radius work (regulated, irreversible)
  • Audit trails — required regardless of how good the AI gets

This is the layer worth investing in now. Practice for humans, not training wheels for AI. Skip it for two years and the muscle atrophies — when the better model arrives you still don't have the habits.

Bottom line → Don't over-engineer for today's bottlenecks — much of L5–L7 will look quaint by 2027. But don't skip the durable layer either. Pick the lightest level that survives this work, knowing the calculator below already accounts for both axes: blast radius forces a floor (governance never goes away), while coordination overhead is what fades as the apprentice becomes the foreman.
🎯 Practical · What to actually hand off to AI in May 2026 Apprentice-carpenter rules · revisit in 6 months
✅ Hand off to AI today
  • Scoped tickets with clear AC — "build endpoint X that returns Y given Z"
  • Refactors with a defined target — extract method, rename, split a file, migrate to a newer API
  • Test writing — given the code, generate unit + integration tests
  • Debugging with a stack trace — paste the error, get the fix candidate
  • Greenfield boilerplate — project setup, DTOs, controllers, OpenAPI schemas, migrations
  • Code-gen from a written plan — once humans approved the plan
  • Documentation drafts — README, ADR first cuts, API examples, code comments where genuinely useful
  • Search and exploration — "where is X defined", "what calls Y", "explain this regex"
  • Cross-language translation — port a Python script to C#, a SQL query to LINQ
✋ Keep human-led (for now)
  • Choosing vendors or architectures — Telnyx vs ACS, REST vs gRPC, monolith vs split. AI can compare; humans decide.
  • Ambiguous requirements — "make the search better" needs a human to define better before AI helps
  • "This looks wrong but I can't say why" intuition — code review, design smell, gut-check on a PR
  • Regulated or irreversible decisions — schema migrations on prod data, auth changes, pricing logic, anything with a compliance shadow
  • Cross-team coordination calls — "is the SettleIT team okay with this contract change?" — AI can draft the message, not make the call
  • Live debugging of subtle / intermittent bugs — race conditions, memory leaks, "works locally, breaks in prod"
  • Final PR review on high-blast-radius code — AI can do a first pass; a human still signs off
  • Strategy and prioritisation — "is this the right feature to build" — not a model question
  • Apologising to a customer — incident comms, tone, judgment. AI drafts; human owns.
Heuristic → If the question is "how do I implement X" → AI. If the question is "should we do X, and what does X even mean" → human. The blast-radius dial in the calculator below is the formalised version of this gut-check.
⚠️ Read this first · How to scope a level

Levels describe a unit of work — which can be a ticket or a whole project.

The lower levels (L0–L2) are ticket-scoped: one developer, hours to a couple of weeks. The higher levels (L3–L7) are project/epic-scoped: multi-ticket efforts that need shared artifacts to keep people aligned. You mix them. A Level-3 project (like AI-Caller) sets a baseline — its AGENTS.md, plan.md, and ticket conventions all exist. Tickets inside the project pick their own level based on their own complexity, durability, and blast radius — which is usually below the project baseline, but occasionally above it for cross-cutting decisions.

🆓 L0 ticket inside an L3 project
Fix a typo in a log message. Bump a NuGet version. Rename a private variable. Just vibe it. AGENTS.md still loads automatically — no plan, ADR, or AC needed for 10 lines.
🗒️ L2 ticket inside an L3 project
A small new endpoint touching one service. Use Plan Mode, get plan approved, implement. No new ADR needed because no new architectural decision is being made.
🏗️ L4 ticket inside an L3 project
Picking the telephony provider (SETTLEIT-10127). A 1-day spike, but cross-cutting and hard to reverse — blast radius alone pushes it to L4. ADR + EARS AC required even though the project sits at L3.
Three dials → Pick the level by asking three questions about this work: (1) Coordination — how many people are involved? (2) Durability — will this outlive the current team or sprint? (3) Blast radius — how bad if it goes wrong? Each dial pushes the level up independently. The calculator below uses all three.

Concrete example

What you write

  • Level 1 + use Shift + Tab Plan Mode in Claude Code (or /plan in Codex) before every non-trivial change.
  • Save the plan as a comment on the Jira ticket OR a .plan.md next to the changed code.
  • Reject the plan, edit it, re-prompt. Then implement.

Explain like you're 15

Before the AI cooks, it has to write the recipe and show you first. You read the recipe, fix what's wrong, then it cooks. The pot has no surprises because the surprises got fixed in the recipe stage when they were cheap to fix.

Skip ifthe work is one obvious change (rename a thing, bump a version, fix a typo).

Explain like you're 15

Building a treehouse with friends. One big blueprint pinned on the wall — everyone has agreed on it. Each kid takes a piece (the door, the ladder, the roof). You meet at the trunk daily to compare what you've built. The blueprint is the truth; arguments are settled by pointing at it.

Watch out fortickets without acceptance criteria; decisions that live only in someone's head; mock-up code drifting from plan.md; "we'll figure it out when we get there" still on the ticket when work starts.

Explain like you're 15

Still building the treehouse — but now you keep a logbook. Every big decision (which wood, which nails) gets a page with the date and who decided. If a new kid joins next week, the logbook tells them everything in 10 minutes instead of two weeks of shadowing.

Concrete example

A new product line. A second team picking up the codebase. SettleIT itself would be at this level if it were rewritten greenfield. Where the spec needs to outlive the team that wrote it.

What you write (on top of L4)

  • Install GitHub Spec Kit CLI (or OpenSpec). Use /specify → /plan → /tasks → /implement.
  • A written constitution — immutable principles every ticket inherits.
  • Drift detection: spec → code traceability checked on every PR.

Explain like you're 15

The blueprint is the machine now. Change the blueprint, the machine rebuilds itself. The actual wood-and-nails is just what falls out of the blueprint — if you lost all the wood tomorrow, the blueprint would tell you exactly how to rebuild. Code is the output, not the input.

Skip ifyour team is <4 people, or the codebase won't outlive the team. Also skip if your work is mostly exploratory / research / R&D — over-spec'ing kills experimentation.

⚠️ Reality check, May 2026

Multi-agent SDLC frameworks (BMAD, MetaGPT, ChatDev) are still experimental. In practice teams hit context drift between agents, hallucination compounding across handoffs, and spend more time debugging the AI-PM↔AI-Dev protocol than writing code. Most large teams do better with L5 (Spec Kit) plus good humans than with agent-arbitration chains. Adopt only if you have a specific reason — e.g. genuinely async distributed teams where file-based handoffs save more than they cost — not because the team is big.

Concrete example

A greenfield platform with genuinely distinct disciplines (PM, architect, frontend, backend, QA) collaborating async across timezones. Or a research environment exploring agentic SDLC itself.

What you write (on top of L5)

  • 12+ agent persona files (PM, architect, dev, QA, SM, UX…).
  • Handoff protocols — agent A reads agent B's output document; explicit role boundaries.
  • Cross-platform config working across Claude Code, Codex, Cursor.

Explain like you're 15

Build a pretend AI company. One AI is the manager. One's the designer. One codes. One tests. They pass work to each other in order, each one reading what the last one wrote. You just manage the AI company; you don't do the actual job. Your skill is hiring, firing, and arbitrating between AI employees.

Skip ifyour team doesn't actually have multiple distinct disciplines, or the disciplines collaborate well already without role-gating.

⚠️ Reality check, May 2026

Most teams who think they need L7 don't. "We work with banks" ≠ "we're regulated like a bank." Before adopting 10–26 checkpoints per bolt and an audit.md appended at every stage, ask Legal/Compliance to point at the specific regulation that requires it. If the answer is "good engineering practice" or "stakeholders feel better" — you want L4 or L5, not this. L7 ceremony on non-regulated work crushes velocity for no defensible reason.

Concrete example

Banking compliance systems. Government records. Healthcare PHI handling. Anything where, in 3 years, an auditor will pull up a feature and ask "prove every decision and every review." Trust & Safety pipelines for AI itself.

What you write (on top of L6)

  • audit.md appended at every stage with timestamp + actor.
  • 10–26 explicit human checkpoints per "bolt" (unit of work).
  • EARS notation on everything. Traceability matrix. Supersedes / depended-by graph.
  • Stage-gate sign-offs that block progress without recorded approval.

Explain like you're 15

Building a hospital, not a treehouse. Every nail is logged. Every plan signed by three different grown-ups. Every change is traceable to a written reason. When the inspector arrives and asks "why was this done?", you don't say "I remember…" — you instantly pull a folder with the full paper trail.

Skip ifyou're not actually regulated. (Most teams who think they are, aren't — check with Legal before adopting this.)
